Umbral Passage

Five hours and fifty-six minutes of a full Moon drifting eastward through the two shadows the Earth casts: the soft penumbra, where sunlight is only partly blocked, and the umbra, where it is blocked altogether and the only light left has been bent through our atmosphere.

Why it goes copper

Nothing inside the umbra receives direct sunlight, so a totally eclipsed Moon ought to vanish. It doesn't. Earth's atmosphere refracts sunlight around the planet's limb and into the shadow, and on the way through it scatters the short wavelengths out — the same Rayleigh scattering that reddens a low Sun.

What reaches the Moon is therefore the light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once, focused into the shadow cone. Totality runs about 10,000 times fainter than a full Moon, and its colour reports on our own air: volcanic aerosol or heavy cloud along the terminator darkens it toward brown, clear air leaves it brick-red with a bright bluish rim where the refracted light has passed through the ozone layer.

Observers grade that appearance on Danjon's five-point scale, estimated at greatest eclipse with the unaided eye.

  • L = 0Very dark; the Moon nearly invisible at mid-totality.
  • L = 1Dark grey or brownish; detail hard to make out.
  • L = 2Deep red or rust, with a darker umbral centre.
  • L = 3Brick-red, often with a brighter yellow rim.
  • L = 4Bright copper-orange; very bright, bluish rim.
